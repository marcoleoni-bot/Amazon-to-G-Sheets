Lesson 1 · Debugging Sheets automation

Wrong answer, or no answer?

Almost every failure in the transfer-order planner is one of two things. Telling them apart takes about thirty seconds, and it decides everything you do next.

The two shapes

When a run comes back wrong, the cell you are staring at is in one of two states. They look identical at a glance and they need opposite responses.

Wrong answer No answer written
What happened The script ran, calculated, and wrote a number you disagree with. The script ran, and the cell was never touched. What you see is whatever was there before.
Whose fault The rules. A threshold, a gate, a target. The environment. Something in the sheet blocked the write.
Fix lives in The Settings tab, or the rule code. The sheet itself — a filter, a named range, a protected cell.

The trap is that a blocked write raises no error. Nothing turns red. The tab looks finished. That is why these took weeks to find: the planner was reporting success over cells it had never managed to change.

The question that separates them

Is this cell holding what the script wrote, or what was there before?

You answer it by clicking the cell and reading the formula bar — not the value in the grid. The value is the same either way. The formula is not.

Here is the actual cell that cracked the September bug. Column P of US TO AWD > FBA, the transfer-cases column:

=ROUNDUP((100-O8)*F8/Q8,0)

That is the old template's formula, from before any of this automation existed. The current script writes something that starts =IF($C8="","",IF(N($F8)<=0,…. One glance at the formula bar said the write had been dropped. The value in the cell said nothing at all.

The one habit worth building

When a cell looks wrong, click it and copy the formula bar. Paste that line to Claude. It is the single highest-information artefact you can send, and it costs five seconds.

The worked example

Symptom: Tactical > FBA came back with no calculated columns at all, three runs in a row, while the other two lanes were fine.

The thing that distinguished the broken lane from the working ones was not in the code. It was this, sitting on the tab:

FILTER over A7:Z498
  showing only rows where column Q is 3 or 7

A filter hides rows. On the run before, exactly two rows out of 491 received their formulas — rows 37 and 154, the only two the filter left visible. A later copy of the same template carried a filter with an empty criteria list, which hides everything, and then not one of the fourteen columns would take a formula.

The two lanes that always worked both carried filters too — with no criteria set. Nothing hidden, nothing lost.

Honest caveat: this is the correlation the evidence in your workbooks shows, and removing the filter fixes it. Google does not advertise this behaviour anywhere prominent, which is exactly the point — the lesson is not "remember the filter rule", it is "a write can vanish without saying so".

Practise

0 of 3 answered

A transfer column shows 0 where you expected 6 cases. You click the cell. The formula bar reads 0. What is this?

A calculated cell always holds a formula. A bare 0 in the formula bar means no formula was ever written — a pasted value, or last run's leftover. Look at the environment, not the rules.

Same column, another row. The formula bar shows a long =IF($C8="",…) formula and the cell reads 0. What is this?

The script's own formula is there and it evaluated to zero. That is a decision, not a failure. Read the Reason column next to it; it will tell you which gate closed.

Two lanes are perfect and one is empty. What does the contrast tell you?

Same code ran over all three. If one tab is broken and two are not, the cause is on the tab — a filter, a protection, hidden rows — not in logic that all three share. Contrast is the cheapest diagnosis there is.

What to send Claude

Four things, in this order. The first two are usually enough.

  1. The exact error text, copied whole. Your message "…these columns would not take a formula, twice over: J, K, O, P…" was what turned a week of guessing into a ten-minute fix: all fourteen columns failing meant the whole tab, not the formulas.
  2. One formula from the formula bar, from a cell that looks wrong.
  3. The file link. Claude can open your sheets and read the filters, protections and named ranges directly.
  4. Which tabs are right and which are wrong. The contrast is half the answer.

What not to lead with: a screenshot of the grid. It shows values, and values are the one thing that looks the same in both failure modes.

Your tangible win

Next time a run looks wrong, before you write to Claude: click one bad cell, read the formula bar, and decide wrong answer or no answer. Say which in your first sentence. That sentence alone removes most of the round trip.
